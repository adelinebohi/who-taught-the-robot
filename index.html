Research notes

I relied on three kinds of sources. Investigative journalism gave the strongest evidence: Billy Perrigo's TIME investigation of OpenAI's Sama contracts in Kenya, and Rebecca Tan and Regine Cabato's Washington Post reporting on Scale AI's Remotasks platform in the Philippines. For numbers, I used Hara et al.'s peer-reviewed study of 3.8 million Mechanical Turk tasks, which found a median wage of about $2 an hour. For framing, I used Gray and Suri's Ghost Work through reviews and summaries, plus the Hicks reading on systems with built-in assumptions. I also used reporting on the Data Labelers Association and the content moderators' alliance for the call to action.

The hardest part was finding the other side of the story. Companies rarely disclose vendors, wages or contract values, so most figures come from reviewed payslips, worker interviews and company rebuttals that disagree. Sama, for example, disputes TIME's numbers, so I included both. Reports also conflict on the amount Scale AI paid to settle its lawsuits, so I said only that the suits were settled. Fairwork's rating of Scale AI reached me through secondary coverage, and I have marked it "as reported." I did not read Ghost Work in full, only reviews and summaries, so I cite it for its framing rather than for new data.

What surprised me most was the scale of the gap in who benefits. The Kenyan filter contracts were worth about $200,000 while OpenAI was reportedly seeking a $29 billion valuation. I came to think a direct comparison would be misleading, because a contract price is not a wage and a valuation is not revenue, so the zine says that outright. I was also surprised that workers are organizing despite the risks, with many members of the Data Labelers Association keeping their membership secret.

Design statement

I chose a digital zine because the format is built to be passed around. A PDF can be shared on a group chat or printed and folded, and the zine tradition of cheap, direct, political print suits a topic about people who are usually left out of the story. The eight-page sequence moves from the problem to the people, conditions, profits, analysis and a call to act, so readers get a clear path from "what is this?" to "what can I do?"

My audience is students and other everyday AI users, people who use chatbots without thinking about how they were built. I did not want to lecture them. The cover opens with a chat bubble they recognize and then shows who stands behind it. I want readers to feel a small jolt of recognition, then curiosity and some discomfort, and to leave with something concrete to do. Page 7 gives five actions, from sharing the zine to asking AI companies about their labeling vendors.

For accessibility, the body text is 11.5 to 12 point, contrast is high (black on cream or yellow), headings follow one consistent hierarchy, and each chart has a text alternative. For honesty, I put a content warning on the trauma section, included Sama's counter-claims, and warned readers not to read a valuation as a pay gap. The yellow, black and red palette and the thick outlines give it a xeroxed zine look while keeping it easy to read.

  1. Pass it on. Share this zine. People can’t demand fairness for work they don’t know exists.
  2. Ask the question. Next time you use a chatbot, ask: whose hands shaped this answer, and what were they paid?
  3. Demand transparency. Press AI companies, and schools that buy AI tools, to disclose their data-labeling vendors and pay standards.
  4. Back the organizers. Follow and amplify worker groups like the Data Labelers Association, Turkopticon and the content moderators’ alliance.
  5. Support real employment rights. Classification rules, minimum pay and mental health protections should follow the work, wherever it’s done.
